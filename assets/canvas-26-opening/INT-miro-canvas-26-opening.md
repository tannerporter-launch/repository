## Section A, content DO and DO NOT

Built from the substance of what the speakers said.

### Section A, content DO

- Frame your AI pitch around the gap between the 10x individual and the 1x company, the way Andrey did. Lead with the organizational gap, not individual productivity, because that is the buyer's real pain.
- Name the three collaboration modes explicitly in strategy docs, human to human, human to agent, agent to agent, and ask which surface each one lands on today. Andrey's "collaboration fracture" is a clean diagnostic for where context is siloed.
- Shift team time toward the 80 percent thinking, 20 percent doing split from the "great inversion." Budget for exploration, context design, judgment, alignment, and direction setting as real work, not overhead.
- Pull hot, sprawling decisions out of Slack threads into one shared surface before deciding, the move Mark made with the FlexFund example. Do this the moment a thread has more than a few people talking past each other.
- Make the agent plan before it acts on any ambiguous request, review the plan, edit it, then approve, exactly as Mark's agentic sidekick did. Never let an agent run a multi-step job on a vague prompt with no visible plan.
- Treat "polish does not mean validated" as a rule, per Kendra. Require feedback, trade-off review, and alternative paths before a beautiful prototype becomes a committed direction.
- Generate three variations for the team to react to instead of defending one direction, because AI-generated options are not precious and people push on them more honestly.
- Save the reasoning, not just the decision. Lukasz's architecture decision record written to the repo as Markdown is the pattern, capture why a choice was made so it survives.
- Enrich prioritization with live customer evidence from channels like Gong, surveys, and Zendesk rather than grooming a static backlog by hand.
- For any regulated or sensitive context, set AI governance before rollout, board classification, prompt redaction, guardrails, approved-model lists, and logging, because bad governance kills adoption faster than any missing feature.

### Section A, content DO NOT

- Do not sell AI as individual speed alone. Jeff and Andrey both showed that individual acceleration without a shared surface produces divergence, not company value.
- Do not let agents validate assumptions in private for hours before a human sees the output, that is how six people end up with six confident, divergent strategies.
- Do not ship monster pull requests faster than the team can align on them, per Lukasz. Skipping the architecture decision just moves the cost to the team that reworks it.
- Do not treat backlog grooming and roadmap upkeep as ongoing manual work if AI can enrich it, that doing is the part to automate.
- Do not add a fifth OKR objective. Jeff said it twice as a joke, but the underlying instruction is real, resist objective sprawl.
- Do not make AI governance so blunt that a single tool call feels like it needs a signed affidavit, or people revert to their old tools.
- Do not send anything internal or private to any hosted service, only ever public URLs, a rule the pipeline's own teardown engine also enforces.

## Section B, form DO and DO NOT

Built from the medium, style, structure, pacing, and delivery of the keynote, not its content.

### Section B, form DO

- Open with an audience-inclusion sequence, the way Amy did with the "who is from where" hand raises. It warms a room and establishes belonging in under a minute.
- Give one memorable role or metaphor early, Amy's "mayor of the office," so the audience has a handle to remember the speaker by.
- Anchor the whole keynote on a single named thesis, the "gap between the 10x individual and the 1x company," and return to it in every segment so the narrative stays coherent across speakers.
- Coin sticky, reusable labels for abstract ideas, "collaboration fracture," "great inversion," "single pane of glass," "anti-terminal." Named concepts travel further than descriptions.
- Number your problems and your solutions, Andrey's four problems and Jeff's what, then how, then who structure. Numbered structure is easy to follow and easy to recall.
- Hand off between speakers by function, customer story, then CEO vision, then product, then builder segments, then scaling, so each voice owns one altitude of the story.
- Show live demos and let them be slightly imperfect. Mark's "you never work with live AI" and Jeff's "sacrifices to the demo gods" turned risk into rapport, and the demos read as real rather than canned.
- Timestamp demos to show freshness, Mark noting Jeff recorded a flow "at 7 p.m. last night," which signals the product is current, not staged weeks ago.
- Use self-deprecating humor to make a real point, Jeff admitting he is the meeting-dominating "hand raiser," which lands a feature and builds trust at once.
- Close on people, not features. Jeff's "team human" and "shared understanding at speed" ending re-centers the emotional message after a dense product tour.

### Section B, form DO NOT

- Do not front-load product announcements. The keynote earned attention with a thesis and customer proof before any feature appeared. Leading with features loses the why.
- Do not let a live demo failure stop the flow. When the sticker did not take, Jeff simply retried and kept talking. Recover in stride, never apologize at length.
- Do not stack unnamed abstractions. Every big idea here got a short label. An unlabeled abstraction is forgotten by the next slide.
- Do not read features as a flat list with no altitude changes. The keynote constantly moved between vision, demo, and joke to reset attention. A monotone product list loses the room.
- Do not overrun without a payoff. Even the fun widgets each tied back to a real collaboration point, nothing was fun for its own sake.

## Section C, procedural templates

Reusable how-tos. Follow the numbered steps.

### Template, how to make a keynote like this

1. Pick one thesis that reframes the buyer's pain at the organizational level, not the individual level.
2. Prove the pain with two or three named customer stories before showing any product.
3. Break the thesis into a small numbered set of problems, four or fewer.
4. Map each product segment to one problem and one altitude, vision, build, or scale.
5. Give every abstract idea a short sticky label you can repeat.
6. Assign one speaker per function so each voice owns a single altitude.
7. Put a live demo in each segment and let it be a little imperfect.
8. Close on people and trust, not on a feature recap alone.

### Template, how to structure a script to recreate this type of video

1. Cold open with audience inclusion and a memorable speaker role.
2. State the timeless truth or thesis in one line ("nobody builds anything great alone," "human ambition has no ceiling").
3. Diagnose the problem with named concepts and a number ("four problems").
4. Transition with "this is where X comes in" to introduce the product answer.
5. Alternate vision, demo, and humor so attention resets every few minutes.
6. End each segment with an explicit handoff to the next speaker and altitude.
7. Recap by listing the pillars, then return to the human message to close.

### Template, how to apply this video's information ordering to other videos

1. Truth, state the durable principle first.
2. Change, name the shift that makes the principle urgent now.
3. Gap, show the cost of not adapting.
4. Named problems, enumerate them.
5. Answer, introduce the product or idea against each problem.
6. Proof, demo it live and cite a customer.
7. Scale, show how it works for one, then for thousands.
8. Human close, end on trust and culture.

### Template, how to apply this video's sequence to sales calls

1. Open by locating the prospect ("seven out of ten conversations end in change management"), naming their pain in their words.
2. State the reframe, individual speed is not company value, to shift the conversation from tools to outcomes.
3. Name the three or four problems the prospect is living, and get agreement on each before pitching.
4. Introduce your answer against each named problem, one at a time.
5. Show one live, current proof point, and cite a comparable customer.
6. Move to scale, how this works past the champion to the whole org, security and adoption included.
7. Close on the relationship and shared understanding, not on a feature list.

### Template, how to apply this video's sequence to marketing sequences

1. Email or post one, the timeless truth and the shift, no product.
2. Email two, the gap and its cost, with a customer story as proof.
3. Email three, the named problems, one message per problem if needed.
4. Email four, the answer mapped to each problem, with a live demo link.
5. Email five, scale and governance, aimed at the economic buyer.
6. Final email, the human close and a single clear call to action.

### Template, how to apply this video's sequence to other contexts

1. Find the durable truth in your domain and state it plainly.
2. Name the recent change that makes the truth urgent.
3. Show the gap between what individuals can do and what the group harnesses.
4. Enumerate a small set of named problems.
5. Offer your answer against each, prove it, then show it scaling.
6. Return to people at the end. The through line is always human first, tool second.
